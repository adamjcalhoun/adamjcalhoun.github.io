About

At Meta Reality Labs, I decode the nervous system at the wrist using electromyography, or EMG, so you can control by intention: no keyboard or touchscreen required.

Before that I spent a decade asking why animals do what they do. As a Simons Foundation research fellow at Princeton, I built machine-learning models that uncover an animal's hidden internal states from its behavior alone — tools now used by labs around the world. My PhD in computational neuroscience at UC San Diego showed that worms forage like information theorists. And before that: pure mathematics at St Andrews, solar physics, econometrics, and software at Intel.

Through-line: careful measurement plus good models turns "random" behavior into something you can predict.

Selected papers

A generic non-invasive neuromotor interface for human–computer interaction
Nature, 2025 · CTRL-labs at Reality Labs
A wristband that reads the faint electrical signals of your muscles and turns them into clicks, gestures and handwriting — and works out of the box for people it has never seen.
Mapping model units to visual neurons reveals population code for social behaviour
Nature, 2024 · Cowley, Calhoun et al., with J. Pillow & M. Murthy
A deep network whose artificial units map one-to-one onto real neurons in a fly's visual system, trained by "knocking out" units just as experiments silence real cells. It reveals how groups of neurons jointly steer courtship.
Unsupervised identification of the internal states that shape natural behavior
Nature Neuroscience, 2019 · with J. Pillow & M. Murthy
A machine-learning method that reads an animal's hidden internal state from behavior alone — predicting a fruit fly's courtship song moment to moment, and revealing which neurons flip it between states.
What is behavior? No seriously, what is it?
iScience, 2023 · with A. El Hady
We surveyed scientists across fields to define "behavior", the thing their fields exist to study. Each person's answer was consistent, but no two fields agreed, and the answers split into at least six distinct kinds.

Skeins, learned

One small network, copied into every bird. It sees only what that bird could sense — the slot it should be holding, the bird ahead, a threat, its own pulse — and it decides how to turn, when to call alarm, and when to leave. Press H to hide these controls, S to hide the traces.

Learning
The rules label every state the flock actually visits, including states the network drove itself into, so handing over early and leaving training on lets it learn its own mistakes.
Two different things. Weights puts an L1 penalty on the parameters and prunes connections — the network keeps using all its units, but each one listens to fewer inputs. Units penalises the hidden activations instead, pushing each layer toward having only a few units awake at a time, which is what changes the traces next door.
Push this too far and the loss will climb — the network is being asked to fly the flock with less. Watching where it breaks is most of the point.

Click any bird to follow it. The left column is what it senses, the right is what it decides, and the pale bar behind each decision is what the rules would have done instead. On the dial, the orange arc is where the leave gate opens and the grey arc where it rejoins.
Drive
Each bird carries a slow phase of its own, one and a half to three and a half minutes. Nothing outside it can see the phase. When it comes round past zero and nothing is frightening the bird, the leave gate opens — so defection is timed from inside, not rolled for.
How far round the dial the second window sits. A bird that has left rejoins when its phase reaches it, so this is both how long a visit lasts and what fraction of the flock is away at any moment.
